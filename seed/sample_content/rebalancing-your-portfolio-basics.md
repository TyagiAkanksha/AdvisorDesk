Rebalancing is the process of periodically buying and selling investments to bring a portfolio back
to its target asset allocation, after market movements have caused it to drift.

## Why Portfolios Drift

Different asset classes grow at different rates. A portfolio that started at a 60% stock / 40% bond
allocation can drift to 70% stocks / 30% bonds after a strong multi-year stock rally, simply because
stocks grew faster than bonds — with no action taken by the investor at all. That drift quietly
increases the portfolio's risk level beyond what was originally intended, often without the investor
noticing until a downturn makes the higher stock weighting painfully apparent.

## Common Rebalancing Approaches

- **Calendar-based rebalancing** reviews and resets the allocation on a fixed schedule — annually or
  semi-annually, for example — regardless of how far the portfolio has drifted.
- **Threshold-based (or "tolerance band") rebalancing** triggers a rebalance whenever an asset class
  drifts beyond a set percentage from its target, such as five percentage points, checked
  periodically rather than on a fixed calendar.
- **Combined approaches** check on a calendar schedule but only actually trade if a threshold has
  also been breached, balancing simplicity against unnecessary trading.

## Rebalancing Mechanics

There are three basic ways to execute a rebalance:

1. **Selling overweight assets and buying underweight ones** directly — the most direct method, but
   one that can trigger taxable capital gains in a taxable account.
2. **Directing new contributions toward underweight assets** rather than splitting new money
   proportionally across the whole portfolio — a lower-cost way to rebalance over time without
   selling anything.
3. **Redirecting dividends and interest** toward underweight positions instead of automatically
   reinvesting them into the same holding that paid them.

## Tax Considerations

Rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts (401(k)s, IRAs) has no immediate tax consequence, since
gains are not taxed until withdrawal (or never, for Roth accounts). Rebalancing in a taxable
account can trigger capital gains — a cost worth weighing against the benefit of staying at the
target allocation. A common practice is to rebalance tax-advantaged accounts more freely, while
using new contributions and threshold-based triggers to nudge taxable accounts back toward target
with less forced selling.

## The Behavioral Case for Rebalancing

Rebalancing is mechanically a "sell high, buy low" discipline: it forces trimming asset classes that
have grown and adding to ones that have lagged, which runs counter to the natural instinct to chase
recent winners. This is precisely why a written rebalancing policy, decided in advance and followed
mechanically, tends to outperform ad hoc, emotionally-driven rebalancing decisions made during
periods of market stress or euphoria.

## How Often Is Enough?

There is no universally optimal rebalancing frequency — too frequent risks unnecessary trading costs
and taxes, while too infrequent allows meaningful drift from the target risk level. Annual or
semi-annual reviews, combined with a threshold trigger for larger moves in between, is a common
starting point for many long-term investors.
