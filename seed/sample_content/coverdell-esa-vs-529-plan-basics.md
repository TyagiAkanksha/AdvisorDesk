A Coverdell Education Savings Account (ESA) is a lesser-known alternative to a 529 plan, sharing the
same basic tax-free-growth-for-education idea but with meaningfully different rules, limits, and
flexibility.

## The Core Similarities

Both account types let earnings grow tax-deferred and allow tax-free withdrawals for qualified
education expenses, and both allow a change of beneficiary to another qualifying family member
without triggering tax consequences.

## Where They Differ

- **Contribution limits.** A Coverdell ESA caps total annual contributions per beneficiary at a
  relatively low, federally fixed dollar amount, unchanged for years and not adjusted for inflation.
  A 529 plan, by contrast, generally allows very large lifetime contribution limits set by each
  state, often well into six figures.
- **Income limits.** Coverdell contribution eligibility phases out at relatively modest income
  levels for the contributor. 529 plans generally have no income limit on who can contribute.
- **Investment flexibility.** Coverdell accounts are typically held at a brokerage and can be
  invested in a wide range of individual securities, mutual funds, and ETFs chosen by the account
  owner. 529 plans restrict investors to a curated menu of portfolios chosen by the plan, with
  changes to investment selections limited to a small number of times per year.
- **Eligible expenses.** Coverdell ESAs have historically allowed a broader range of K-12 expenses
  (tutoring, computers, and other supplies) beyond just tuition, though 529 plans have expanded to
  cover more K-12 costs in recent years, narrowing this gap.
- **Age limits.** Coverdell contributions generally must stop once the beneficiary turns 18, and
  funds generally must be used by age 30 or rolled to another eligible family member, unless the
  beneficiary has special needs. 529 plans have no such age restriction.

## Why 529 Plans Are More Widely Used

Because of the much higher contribution limits, absence of an income phase-out, and state tax
deduction opportunities many states offer, 529 plans have become the dominant college savings
vehicle for most families. Coverdell ESAs tend to appeal to a narrower group: families who want
maximum control over individual investment selections, or who value the slightly broader K-12
expense definition Coverdell accounts have historically offered.

## Can You Use Both?

Yes — there is no rule against contributing to both a 529 plan and a Coverdell ESA for the same
beneficiary in the same year, as long as each account's own contribution rules are respected
separately. Some families use a Coverdell for its investment flexibility on a smaller amount, while
directing the bulk of college savings into a 529 plan for the higher limits.

## The Bottom Line

For most families, a 529 plan's higher contribution ceiling and lack of an income limit make it the
more practical primary vehicle, with a Coverdell ESA occasionally used as a smaller, more
flexibly-invested supplement rather than a replacement.
